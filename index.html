Executive BLUF

The most important near-term issue is the compression of three rural policy and funding decisions into the next two weeks. Oklahoma's PRIMS Project 695 school-health grant closes September 11, CMS closes comments on the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule September 14, and comments on the Medicaid provider-tax rule close September 21. The PFS proposal includes a direct RHC opportunity by making Diabetes Self-Management Training and Medical Nutrition Therapy stand-alone billable RHC visits paid through the All-Inclusive Rate, while the provider-tax rule carries a CMS-estimated $246 billion reduction in federal Medicaid expenditures over 2026 through 2035.

At the same time, federal RHTP implementation is increasingly validating a regional operating model. New York's September 4 award emphasizes integrated rural networks and coordinated transitions, Michigan is investing in interoperability, broadband, telehealth and remote monitoring, and Oklahoma's own RHTP rules favor partnerships and require sustainability beyond the grant. UnitedHealthcare's October authorization reductions and fall rural initiatives create a separate opportunity to measure payer friction as part of effective reimbursement. For leadership, September should be treated as a design and decision month: influence the rules, baseline payer burden, and prioritize shared capabilities that remain valuable after RHTP funding ends.

One Action This Week

Build one September rural decision sheet with five fields for each item: deadline, facilities or partners affected, estimated financial or operational exposure, accountable owner, and requested executive decision. Start with September 11, September 14 and September 21, then add the October UnitedHealthcare authorization change. This converts a crowded policy calendar into a manageable operating agenda.

Top 5 Developments Today

1. Oklahoma RHTP moves from general opportunity to near-term partnership decisions

OklahomaSep 11Oklahoma's active RHTP funding page lists PRIMS Project 695 as due September 11 at 11:59 p.m. CT. Eligible PK-12 districts can receive support for up to 90% of salary and benefits for new health or mental-health professionals who can bill Medicaid, including nurses, counselors, therapists and speech-language pathologists. Oklahoma's Rural Regional Reorientation program also allows grants of up to $4 million and gives preference to partnerships, with an explicit expectation that funded models be sustainable without relying on additional RHTP money.

Why it matters: the state is now funding operating models that connect workforce, Medicaid billing and regional partnerships rather than only equipment. Hospitals and RHCs can add value through referral pathways, credentialing support, telehealth, clinical escalation and shared workforce relationships without becoming the direct applicant in every case. Action: identify rural school districts and regional partners where a health-system relationship could make the grant more clinically durable or improve continuity between school-based care, primary care and hospital services.

2. CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule comments close September 14, with a concrete RHC revenue opportunity

MedicareSep 14CMS proposes to recognize Diabetes Self-Management Training and Medical Nutrition Therapy as qualified preventive services covered and paid at the RHC All-Inclusive Rate as stand-alone billable visits. CMS also proposes conforming changes that continue RHC and FQHC telehealth flexibilities through December 31, 2027, and the rule contains broader Medicare Shared Savings Program changes intended to increase participation in two-sided risk.

Why it matters: DSMT and MNT could become an ordinary Medicare reimbursement pathway for rural chronic-disease management rather than a service whose cost is merely absorbed into the RHC cost structure. That creates a useful bridge between RHTP-funded program development and post-grant sustainability. Decision: determine whether comments should specifically support the RHC proposal, request clarification on operational details, or address rural ACO and telehealth provisions before September 14.

3. Medicaid provider-tax rule should be modeled as part of a single 2028 to 2031 downside case

MedicaidSep 21CMS-2452-P would implement statutory changes to the indirect hold-harmless test for healthcare-related taxes and codify restrictions on new and increased provider taxes. CMS states that 49 states and the District of Columbia use at least one healthcare-related tax and estimates the proposed rule would reduce federal Medicaid expenditures by $246 billion from 2026 through 2035. Comments are due September 21.

Why it matters: provider taxes, state-directed payments, DSH and base Medicaid reimbursement are connected components of hospital financing. A state response to one federal restriction can alter the others. Action: build one facility-level Medicaid resilience model that combines provider-tax exposure, state-directed-payment exposure, DSH, Medicaid volume, uncompensated care and recurring RHTP costs. The useful board question is which programs become unsustainable first if supplemental financing is materially less favorable by 2031.

4. Rural Transformation awards are increasingly funding networks rather than isolated facilities

Rural StrategySep 4CMS announced September 4 that New York is directing $76 million toward regional healthcare networks, integrated care transitions and technology-enhanced primary care. Michigan is directing $25 million toward digital modernization, data exchange, broadband, telehealth and remote patient monitoring. Rhode Island is using $5.48 million to build rural healthcare career pathways through 14 local education agencies and schools.

Why it matters: the federal implementation pattern now spans clinical access, workforce, interoperability and regional governance. The common denominator is capability distributed across geography. Action: require every major Oklahoma Budget Period 2 concept to answer three questions: how many rural organizations benefit, what capability is shared rather than duplicated, and who pays the recurring expense after federal funding ends?

5. UnitedHealthcare's fall rural initiatives create a real-world test of payer friction

PayerOct 1UnitedHealthcare will eliminate 30% of its remaining prior authorization requirements beginning October 1 across commercial, Medicare Advantage, Community Plan and individual exchange products. Separately, the company says its rural initiative will expand payment acceleration and exemptions from most medical prior authorization requirements to approximately 1,500 rural hospitals and associated practitioners nationwide, including all Critical Access Hospitals, during fall 2026.

Why it matters: this creates an opportunity to measure whether a payer's administrative behavior changes the true economics of the contract. Action: establish a September baseline for authorizations per 1,000 encounters, denial rates, appeal hours, days to service and days to payment. Then calculate effective reimbursement after administrative cost and compare performance after the changes take effect.

Executive Interpretation

The most durable rural strategy emerging from current public policy is to preserve local access where geography matters and centralize complexity where scale matters. RHTP is increasingly funding regional infrastructure, CMS is creating possible recurring reimbursement pathways for RHC chronic-disease services, Medicaid financing is tightening, and payer burden is becoming measurable. The best transformation investments should reduce structural operating friction and remain economically useful after federal grant funding ends.
