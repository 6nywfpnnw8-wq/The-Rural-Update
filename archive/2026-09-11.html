Top 5 Developments

1

Oklahoma PRIMS school-health grant closes tonight

OklahomaAction

Oklahoma's Rural Health Transformation funding page lists the PRIMS Project 695 competitive grant deadline as 11:59 p.m. CT today, September 11. Eligible PK-12 districts can receive support for up to 90% of salary and benefits for new health or mental-health professionals capable of Medicaid billing, including nurses, counselors, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists.

Why it matters: this is an immediate opportunity to connect rural schools with reimbursable health services and durable referral pathways. Hospitals and RHCs can add value through clinical escalation, referral design, telehealth, and continuity of care even when the school district is the direct applicant.
Executive question: Are there any rural school partnerships where a hospital or RHC relationship would materially improve access or continuity and should be resolved before tonight's deadline?
2

HHS awards nearly $25 million directly to 132 small rural hospitals

Rural PolicyNew

HHS, through HRSA, announced September 10 that 132 hospitals across 13 states will receive nearly $25 million through the Rural Hospital Provider Assistance Program. The program targets eligible small rural hospitals with no more than 50 acute-care beds and Medicare wage index values below 0.90, with funding intended to maintain medical workforce, essential services, and local access.

Why it matters: this is targeted stabilization funding tied explicitly to structural payment disadvantage, not transformation capital. It reinforces federal recognition that some small rural hospitals cannot solve low-volume and wage-index economics through efficiency alone.
Executive question: Which recurring financial pressures in small rural hospitals are structural enough that they require payment-policy correction rather than another temporary grant?
3

RHC telehealth billing changes October 1

MedicareAction

CMS confirmed September 10 that, beginning October 1, RHCs and FQHCs furnishing distant-site telehealth must stop using the single HCPCS code G2025 and instead bill the individual CPT or HCPCS code describing the service with modifier 93 for qualifying audio-only services or modifier 95 for real-time audio-video services. CMS lists the CY 2026 RHC and FQHC distant-site telehealth payment rate at $97.53.

Why it matters: this is a near-term revenue-cycle conversion affecting coding, charge capture, claim edits, documentation, staff training, and payer testing. A technically valid telehealth encounter can still become a denial if billing workflows remain configured around G2025 after September 30.
Executive question: Are every RHC's telehealth charge master, coding edits, documentation guidance, and clearinghouse rules ready for individual-code billing on October 1?
4

Medicare and Medicaid policy deadlines remain compressed into the next ten days

Federal PolicyAction

CMS closes comments on the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule on September 14. The proposal includes stand-alone RHC payment for Diabetes Self-Management Training and Medical Nutrition Therapy, telehealth changes, remote-monitoring policy, and Shared Savings Program revisions. The House Energy and Commerce Health Subcommittee holds a September 15 hearing on Medicare provider payment and healthcare cybersecurity. Comments on CMS's Medicaid provider-tax rule close September 21, with CMS estimating $246 billion in lower federal Medicaid expenditures over 2026 through 2035.

Why it matters: the decisions span recurring RHC reimbursement, physician economics, cyber investment, and the future structure of hospital Medicaid financing. Rural leadership should treat them as one strategic policy window rather than separate regulatory events.
Executive question: Which provisions require formal advocacy now, and which require an operating or financial contingency plan regardless of the final policy outcome?
5

AI and cyber capability are moving deeper into regulated and public-sector infrastructure

AI & CyberWatch

OpenAI and the U.S. General Services Administration announced September 10 a multi-year agreement providing broad federal, state, local, and tribal government access to AI tools with expanded support for public-sector cyber defenders. Separately, Anthropic reported additional misuse of advanced AI for cyber-espionage and biological and weapons-related research, according to Reuters.

Why it matters: advanced AI is becoming both defensive infrastructure and an offensive force multiplier. Healthcare organizations should expect the capability gap between well-governed and poorly governed cyber programs to widen, while agentic systems require controls comparable to privileged enterprise identities.
Executive question: Is AI cyber capability being incorporated into enterprise security strategy with independent logging, least-privilege access, rapid credential revocation, and clear human accountability?
