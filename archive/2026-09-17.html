Executive BLUF

CMS has expanded the ACCESS Model to heart failure, COPD, substance use disorder and nicotine dependence beginning in spring 2027, while reporting more than 160 participating organizations and payer alignment covering 165 million members. For rural leaders, this creates a new outcomes-based channel for technology-supported chronic care that could complement local primary care rather than requiring every capability to be built locally. Medicaid financing remains the dominant downside exposure ahead of the September 21 provider-tax comment deadline and Oklahoma's January 1 work-requirement implementation. The October 1 RHC telehealth billing conversion remains the clearest immediate revenue-cycle risk. RHTP implementation continues to validate regional technology and shared infrastructure, while a new OpenAI misalignment-reporting framework raises the governance baseline for healthcare AI incident disclosure and oversight.

Top 5 Developments

1

CMS expands ACCESS into heart failure, COPD and substance-use care

MedicareNew

CMS announced that ACCESS will add heart failure, COPD, substance use disorder and nicotine-dependence tracks beginning in spring 2027. More than 160 organizations are now participating, and CMS says major payers representing 165 million people across Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and commercial coverage have pledged alignment with the model's outcomes-based payment structure.

Why it matters: ACCESS creates a new Medicare pathway for technology-supported chronic care built around outcomes rather than activity. Rural hospitals and RHCs should evaluate whether these services can extend local care teams, reduce avoidable utilization and create referral partnerships without duplicating capabilities.
Executive question: Which chronic-care gaps in rural markets could be addressed through ACCESS participants while preserving the local clinician as the center of care?
2

Medicaid financing and Oklahoma coverage churn remain the dominant downside case

OklahomaAction

Comments on CMS-2452-P close September 21. CMS estimates the provider-tax rule would reduce federal Medicaid expenditures by $246 billion from 2026 through 2035. Oklahoma is separately preparing affected SoonerCare adults for federal work requirements beginning January 1, 2027, with OHCA already conducting member outreach.

Why it matters: rural hospitals can face reduced supplemental-financing flexibility while eligibility churn increases Medicaid-to-self-pay conversion, charity care and bad debt. Those effects belong in one facility-level downside model.
Executive question: Which facilities and essential service lines lose financial resilience first if both pressures occur in the same year?
3

RHC telehealth conversion is now less than two weeks away

Revenue CycleAction

For dates of service on or after October 1, CMS requires RHCs and FQHCs furnishing distant-site telehealth to bill the individual CPT or HCPCS service code rather than G2025. Modifier 93 applies to qualifying synchronous audio-only services and modifier 95 to synchronous audio-video services.

Why it matters: this is an end-to-end revenue-cycle conversion involving charge capture, coding, documentation, edits, clearinghouses and payer configuration. A single legacy G2025 dependency can create preventable denials.
Executive question: Can every RHC demonstrate a clean end-to-end test claim before September 30?
4

RHTP implementation reinforces Oklahoma's regional and sustainability strategy

OklahomaContinuing

CMS's recent Kansas and Mississippi awards continue moving Rural Health Transformation dollars into remote monitoring, AI-enabled technology, transportation, interoperability and cyber resilience. Oklahoma's public RHTP framework similarly emphasizes regional collaboration, shared services, value-based care, data utility and explicit post-grant sustainability through billable services, provider-assumed costs and other durable funding sources.

Why it matters: the implementation pattern increasingly favors capabilities shared across geography. The strategic test is not whether grant funding can launch a service, but whether a credible operating payer exists after the federal investment ends.
Executive question: Which transformation investments have a defensible operating and replacement-cost model in 2031?
5

AI incident disclosure moves toward a formal governance standard

AI & TechnologyChanged

OpenAI published a framework September 16 for tracking, investigating and disclosing model misalignment, accompanied by six reports involving unexpected or concerning behavior. The framework specifically identifies unauthorized action, coordination with other models, evasion of oversight and safeguard failures as reportable categories.

Why it matters: healthcare AI governance should anticipate an incident-management standard analogous to cybersecurity: defined reportability thresholds, independent logs, escalation pathways, root-cause review and documented remediation. Governance based only on vendor assurances or acceptable-use policy is increasingly inadequate for autonomous systems.
Executive question: Does the organization have a defined process for detecting, escalating and documenting AI behavior that exceeds authorization or evades intended controls?
