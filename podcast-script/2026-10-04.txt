This is The Rural Update for Sunday, October fourth, twenty twenty-six. The central theme this morning is infrastructure. Rural transformation is increasingly linking hospitals, EMS, digital systems, workforce, maternal care, payment, and artificial intelligence into one operating architecture. The practical question for rural leaders is no longer whether these pieces matter individually. It is whether they are being governed as an integrated system that can remain viable after temporary funding ends.

This matters beyond South Dakota. Rural regionalization depends on transport. Hospital strategy and EMS strategy therefore cannot be developed separately.

Third, the GLOBE Medicare Part B drug model requires preparation, but the timeline needs to be understood correctly. The model applies to selected high-spend Part B drugs in oncology, rheumatology, immunology, ophthalmology, and endocrinology, with certain exceptions. Fourth, clinical AI governance is becoming more operational. Procurement, evidence review, cybersecurity, monitoring, reimbursement, and clinical accountability should reflect that difference.

TOP DEVELOPMENT ONE: EMS IS BECOMING CORE RURAL HEALTH INFRASTRUCTURE

CMS announced seven point two million dollars in Rural Health Transformation funding for twenty-five South Dakota projects led by ambulance services, hospitals, community organizations, education partners, and statewide EMS support organizations.

The projects include ambulance-based telemedicine, expanded EMS training, faster access to patient information, communications modernization, and quality improvement.

The strategic implication is larger than the award amount.

For years, rural hospital strategy has often been discussed primarily in terms of what services remain inside the hospital. But as rural delivery models become more regional, the connecting infrastructure becomes equally important.

A patient with stroke, trauma, sepsis, obstetric emergency, or cardiac disease may begin care locally but require movement to a higher-capability facility. The quality of that model depends on early stabilization, transport availability, destination decisions, communication with the receiving hospital, and timely return to local care when the higher level of service is no longer required.

Why it matters: service regionalization without transport capacity can increase access risk even if the receiving center has excellent clinical capability.

What to watch or do: add EMS to rural affiliate capability assessments. Measure ambulance availability, response and transfer times, destination patterns, bypass behavior, equipment, staffing, communications, clinical data exchange, and the frequency with which transport availability delays transfer.

TOP DEVELOPMENT TWO: OKLAHOMA’S DIGITAL FUNDING WINDOW CREATES AN EXECUTION OPPORTUNITY

Oklahoma’s Rural Health Transformation funding page lists the OHCA HIE and EHR Assistance Year One application as active through December thirty-first.

The program is designed for eligible rural providers seeking statewide HIE participation or assistance implementing, replacing, optimizing, or expanding certified EHR systems.

This should be treated as more than an information-technology grant.

Interoperability increasingly affects the entire operating model. A rural provider’s ability to exchange clinical information influences transfers, referrals, care transitions, quality reporting, payer connectivity, electronic prior authorization, population health, and value-based contracting.

The opportunity also aligns with the federal interoperability deadlines approaching in twenty twenty-seven. Rural affiliates that have aging systems, incomplete interfaces, weak HIE participation, or uncertain API capability need a current-state inventory now.

Why it matters: temporary funding can help remove capital barriers that otherwise leave small rural providers behind larger systems technologically.

What to watch or do: complete a facility-level matrix covering EHR vendor and version, certified status, HIE connectivity, interface gaps, electronic prior authorization capability, payer API readiness, cybersecurity needs, replacement timeline, training requirements, and estimated recurring cost after assistance ends.

TOP DEVELOPMENT THREE: PART B DRUG PAYMENT COMPLEXITY IS INCREASING

CMS has finalized the Global Benchmark for Efficient Drug Pricing, or GLOBE, Model.

The model will assess rebates for certain drugs payable under Original Medicare Part B when prices exceed benchmarks derived from economically comparable countries. CMS says the model launches January first, twenty twenty-seven.

Selected drugs will come from oncology, rheumatology, immunology, ophthalmology, and endocrinology therapeutic classes, subject to program rules and exceptions, and must meet specified Medicare spending thresholds.

For rural hospitals, the important issue is not simply the policy objective. High-cost physician-administered drugs can create significant acquisition-cost and working-capital exposure. Even modest changes in payment mechanics, beneficiary coinsurance, utilization, or timing can matter in low-margin organizations.

Why it matters: drug policy is becoming another layer of reimbursement complexity alongside 340B, ASP-based payment, sequestration, Medicare Advantage contracting, and site-of-service policy.

What to watch or do: build a Part B drug exposure file by facility. Identify high-spend drugs, annual acquisition cost, Original Medicare volume, payer mix, 340B status where applicable, reimbursement methodology, beneficiary liability, and cash-collection timing. Then compare that list with final GLOBE drug-selection rules as CMS implementation develops.

OKLAHOMA AND RURAL-SPECIFIC VIEW

Oklahoma’s first-year Rural Health Transformation award is more than two hundred twenty-three million dollars. The state’s active funding opportunities show how quickly the program is moving from planning into implementation.

Two principles should guide rural governance.

First, temporary funding should build permanent capability rather than permanent expense without a revenue source. Every major project should identify post-grant staffing, software, licensing, maintenance, connectivity, equipment replacement, reimbursement, expected volume, and value-based revenue or savings.

Second, rural transformation should be evaluated at the network level. An EHR upgrade at one facility, telemedicine at another, an EMS investment in a third community, and a workforce program somewhere else may look unrelated when reviewed as individual grants. They can create substantially more value when designed as components of a regional delivery architecture.

For affiliate networks, that means mapping what must remain local, what can be shared, what should be regionalized, and what infrastructure connects those layers safely.
